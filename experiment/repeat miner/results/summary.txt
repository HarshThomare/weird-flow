Repeat miner

Core question: does repetition alone separate weird gates from everything else, and at what granularity?

Ground truth is DualGate symbols in Flexo objects and jmpreg symbols in TAE binaries, recorded before strip --strip-all. The miner sees only the stripped .text bytes. A repeated cluster is an exact feature key with count >= 2. Recall is the fraction of ground-truth gates whose instructions are at least 50% covered. recall_nonop drops nop instructions from that fraction. recall_nonop_90 requires 90% of the non-nop instructions. Purity is the fraction of repeated-cluster members whose span overlaps a gate. FPR is the fraction of instructions outside every gate that still fall in a repeated cluster (on a binary with no gates, that is just instruction coverage).

What the numbers say about granularity:
On Flexo test -O2 the bodies are mostly a shared nop sled (recall 1.00 with nops in the denominator, non-nop recall 1.00, mean logic coverage 0.97, 90% bar 0.88).
Dropping nops from the coverage test does not by itself erase recall, so some non-nop bytes are shared too.
Deleting nop-only clusters moves all-insn recall from 1.00 to 0.00 and leaves non-nop recall at 1.00.
The 50% bar over every byte, nops included, is what the sled was carrying. The non-nop instructions stay covered by repeated logic, and abstraction is what pushes that coverage to the whole gate.
Same binary, abstract features: basic-block recall 0.25 non-nop 0.25; CFG-edge recall 0.00 non-nop 0.00; 4-gram recall 1.00 non-nop 1.00; 6-gram raw recall 1.00 non-nop 0.62 purity 0.44 versus abstract purity 0.43.
Basic blocks are coarser than the repeated fragment: a whole gate-plus-fallthrough rarely matches another gate, so block keys miss gates that n-grams hit.
Abstraction raises non-nop recall: gates share an instruction shape but not concrete registers or immediates.
TAE -O2 n-gram-6 raw recall 1.00 non-nop 1.00 purity 0.77 fpr 0.01 clusters 59. Abstract: recall 1.00 non-nop 1.00 purity 0.02 fpr 1.00 clusters 140.
Constant abstraction lowers TAE purity. The decode table is thousands of `mov immediate; ret` sites; once the immediate is erased they cluster, and they are not jmpreg gates.
The jmpreg gates themselves are one repeated `jmp` plus a fixed pad, so raw repetition recalls them without any weirdness test. That is recall of a clone, not a test that the clone is weird.

At this size the approximate method is the slower one. It earns its cost only when near-duplicates must count as the same gate. Identical clones do not need it.

Answer
Repetition alone does not separate weird gates from everything else. On Flexo test -O2, abstract 6-grams recall 1.00 of the DualGates and also cover 0.96 of the instructions outside them (purity 0.43). On TAE the same feature recalls 1.00 of the jmpreg clones, with purity 0.02 after constant abstraction versus 0.77 raw. The highest abstract 6-gram coverage on a hard negative is 1.00. The match that fires is an n-gram of the shared piece: a nop sled, a jmp pad, or an abstracted instruction shape. Basic blocks are coarser than that piece on Flexo, and CFG edges add little on this straight-line code. Register abstraction recovers renamed copies and also welds the TAE decode table and the template instantiations into false clusters. A shuffle breaks the n-gram. A regular nop gap does not: it becomes part of the repeated key. A shared sled does the same. Exact clustering is the right tool for identical clones. MinHash is what still joins a 10% edit, and it costs more at 65536 items. A second build is a filter on keys the first binary already repeats, not a weirdness test.
